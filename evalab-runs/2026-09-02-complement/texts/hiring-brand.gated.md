Every engineer here deploys in their first week. Not a toy change, either — something a customer will notice.

We do this because the fastest way to learn a codebase is to be responsible for a piece of it, and because a two-month onboarding ramp tells you more about a company's fear than about its standards. We write things down, and nobody grades the prose. Design notes before big work, postmortems after outages, and a decision log so the next person doesn't have to reconstruct why we picked Postgres over [X] in [year]. The point is that the reasoning outlives the person who had it. Code review is a conversation between two people who both want the change to land, so we answer within [four hours] during the working day, and a reviewer who thinks a change is fine is expected to say "looks fine, ship it" and let it go out.

On-call is one week in [six]. If a rotation wakes you up twice, that's a bug in our system, not a fact of the job. Whoever got paged gets the next day to go fix the cause, and that work counts as work.

We disagree in writing, in threads other people can read, rather than in hallways. Once a call is made we commit to it.

The objection stays in the log, though, and if you turn out to have been right, we say so out loud. You also won't find hero culture here, or crunch as a plan, or an interview that asks you to recall a graph algorithm from memory. We give you problems close to the ones you'd actually work on, and we pay for the long ones.
