We started a mentorship program on my team last quarter. Here is what it looked like and what I got wrong.

The trigger was a pattern in exit interviews. Three of the four engineers who left in 2025 said some version of "I didn't know what getting better looked like here." We had career ladders. Nobody read them, because a ladder tells you the destination and not the road.

The design is deliberately small. Pairs meet for 45 minutes every other week. The mentee owns the agenda and sends it the day before. If they send nothing, the meeting is cancelled, no guilt. That last rule did more work than anything else, because it killed the standing meeting that survives on politeness after it stops being useful.

I matched people by the skill gap they named, not by tenure. Two of our strongest mentors are mid-level engineers who happen to be very good at a specific thing: one at reading unfamiliar code fast, one at writing a design doc that gets read. Seniority and teaching ability are different axes.

What I got wrong: I made it opt-in for mentees and opt-out for mentors, which meant senior people who were already overloaded got a calendar invite they felt bad about declining. Two of them quietly stopped preparing. I switched it to opt-in on both sides in month two and lost three pairs, which was the honest number.

Six months in, eight pairs are still meeting. The metric I actually watch is whether mentees are sending agendas, because that is the part that fails first when the program stops mattering to someone.
