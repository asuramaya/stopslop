# What a year of remote work actually taught us

We went remote in March and told ourselves it was temporary. It wasn't, and the lessons only showed up once we stopped treating it as a stopgap.

The first thing that broke was the assumption that a meeting is free. In an office, pulling six people into a room costs a walk down a hallway. On video, it costs six calendar blocks and the twenty minutes of scattered attention on either side. We cut our recurring meetings from eleven a week to four, and nobody asked for the other seven back.

The second thing that broke was onboarding. Our old process relied on a new hire absorbing context by sitting near people. Remotely, that context simply didn't transfer. Two engineers who joined in June took roughly twice as long to ship their first meaningful change as anyone hired the year before. We fixed it by writing things down: an architecture overview, a decision log, a "who to ask about what" page. Boring documents, big effect.

The third lesson was about time zones. We hired across five of them because we could, then spent months discovering that a four-hour overlap window makes some kinds of collaboration nearly impossible. Pair debugging works. Open-ended design discussion does not. We now cluster teams within three hours of each other and accept the smaller hiring pool.

What we did not learn: whether remote work is better. It's different, and the differences are specific. Most of the pain came from running office processes over video and expecting the same results.
