Why we're funding developer relations this year

We shipped an API two years ago and treated documentation as a release chore. The pattern that followed was consistent: people signed up, got through the quickstart, and then stalled somewhere in the second hour. Support tickets told us where — auth token refresh, pagination, and the webhook retry semantics we never wrote down properly. Every one of those tickets was a question the docs should have answered before it was asked.

So we're hiring two people into developer relations, reporting into engineering rather than marketing. That reporting line is deliberate. We want them filing pull requests against the SDKs, not producing conference decks.

The job as we've scoped it: rewrite the reference docs against real integrations, maintain the example applications so they run on current versions, and sit in the community channels where people are actually stuck. If a question comes up three times, it becomes a doc page. If it comes up ten times, it becomes an API change.

We're measuring this by time-to-first-successful-call and by the share of support volume that turns out to be a documentation gap. Those numbers are currently 90 minutes median and roughly 40 percent. We'd like the first under 20 minutes and the second under 15 by the end of next year. If neither moves, the investment was wrong and we'll say so.

Developer relations gets described as a growth function. For us it's closer to quality assurance done from the outside in — the part of the product our own engineers cannot see, because they already know how it works.
