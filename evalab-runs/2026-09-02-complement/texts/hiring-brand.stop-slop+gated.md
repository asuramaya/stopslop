Engineering Culture

You ship in your first week. A backend engineer who joined us last spring merged a change to our billing retry logic on day three, and it reached production that afternoon behind a flag she owned. We keep teams to six people or fewer, and each team owns its services from the design doc through the pager. If you write it, you carry it, and you decide when to wake someone else up. Code review runs on a two-hour clock during working hours, and we read for correctness and for whether the next person can follow the change. A linter settles brace style before you open a pull request, so it stays out of review.

We also write things down.

Each project starts with a two-page document naming the problem, the option we picked, and the ones we threw out. Those documents live in the repo, so you can pull up the reasoning behind a 2021 decision and see who signed it. About 40 percent of our engineering time goes to work customers don't see, mostly migrations and the test suite. Our staff engineers defend that number in planning, and they win most of the time.

We hold one fifteen-minute standup and one planning session per two-week cycle. You defend the rest of your calendar. When something breaks, we write a postmortem naming the system and the gap in our tooling. We leave out the name of the engineer who pushed the change, then spend the next sprint closing the gap.

Bring hard questions to the interview. We answer them.
