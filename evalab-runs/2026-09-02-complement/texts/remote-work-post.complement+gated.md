**A year of remote work, and what it actually cost us**

We went fully remote in March last year. Here's what held up and what didn't.

The productivity panic never materialized. Story points per sprint stayed flat for the first two quarters, then rose slightly. What dropped was the number of people who knew why a decision got made. In the office, three engineers overhearing a hallway argument absorbed the reasoning for free. Remote, that context lives in one Slack thread that nobody scrolls back to.

So we started writing decision records. Short ones, a paragraph, filed next to the code. Onboarding time for new hires dropped from six weeks to about three.

Meetings got worse before they got better. By month four we had a standing calendar of eleven recurring syncs, most of them created to replace a conversation that used to take ninety seconds. We deleted seven. Nothing broke.

The real cost showed up in the junior engineers. Senior people had years of relationships to draw on and did fine. Someone six months into their first job had no way to ask a small, embarrassing question without booking a video call. We paired them with a named mentor and required two hours of screen-share time a week. Retention in that group went from two departures in eight months to zero in the next ten.

Time zones remain unsolved. We have people in Lisbon and Denver, and the four-hour overlap is genuinely tight. We've stopped pretending a clever tool will fix it and now just hire in bands.

Would we go back? No. But it took real work to make it not worse.
