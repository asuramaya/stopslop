# What I got wrong starting a mentorship program

Two engineers left my team last spring. In both exit conversations the same complaint came up in different words: they were shipping tickets and learning nothing. So I started a mentorship program, and the first version of it failed.

I paired people and walked away. Six pairs, a Slack channel, a doc explaining the goals. Four of the six never met a second time, and the two that worked were pairs who already ate lunch together.

The second version is smaller and more annoying to run. It has three rules.

Pairing is opt-in on both sides, and I ask the senior first. A senior who agrees out of obligation cancels twice and then the pair dies quietly.

Meetings go on the calendar. Thirty minutes, recurring, on the mentor's calendar rather than the mentee's. If it isn't scheduled it doesn't happen, and I stopped pretending otherwise.

Each pair picks one concrete thing for the quarter, which rules out "grow as an engineer." One of ours was "review a PR without me correcting it afterward." Another was "run the Thursday deploy alone." You can tell whether it happened.

What I didn't expect was how much the mentors got out of it. Two of them said that explaining our queue setup was the first time they understood parts of it themselves. One rewrote a runbook because a mentee's question exposed that it had been wrong for a year.

Five months in, nobody has left. That proves nothing on its own, but I would rather have it than not.
