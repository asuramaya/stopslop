A year of remote work at a 40-person software company

We went remote in March, expecting to go back in June. We didn't.

The first thing that broke was not productivity. Ticket throughput held steady for eight months and then dipped about 15% in the ninth, which is roughly when three of our senior engineers told their managers they felt disconnected from the work. By the time output moved, the thing that caused it had been true for a while. That lag is the part I'd warn other companies about: the metric you watch is the last thing to tell you anything.

Meetings expanded to fill the space that hallways used to occupy. By August the average engineer had 11 recurring meetings a week, up from 4. We killed every standing meeting for two weeks and reinstated only the ones somebody asked for. Six of them nobody asked for.

Written communication turned out to be a skill our company had never hired for or taught. Decisions that used to survive on shared context now needed a paragraph of explanation, and the people who wrote well accumulated influence quickly — some of them deserved it more than others. We started requiring a one-page written proposal before any architecture discussion, which killed about a third of the discussions before they happened.

Onboarding is still broken. New hires in 2021 took about 10 weeks to their first meaningful commit, against 5 weeks for the 2019 cohort. A dedicated buddy cut it to 7.

We're staying remote. The case for it is real, and so is the bill.
