What a year of remote work taught us

We went remote expecting a scheduling problem. It was a writing problem.

In the office, a decision could survive on tone of voice and the fact that four people happened to be at the same whiteboard. Remote, none of that carries. The first three months, our defect rate climbed and nobody could explain why. When we dug in, most of the bad tickets traced back to specs that had been "agreed" in a call with no written outcome. So we made a rule: if a decision isn't in the doc, it didn't happen. Defects came back down over the next quarter, though I won't pretend the rule was the only thing that changed.

Meetings shrank on their own once writing carried the load. We went from about 14 hours of recurring meetings a week per engineer to 5. The hours that survived were the ones where people actually disagreed.

The thing we got wrong was onboarding. Our first two remote hires took roughly twice as long to ship independently as anyone hired in the office, and one of them left inside six months. Documentation didn't fix it. What helped was assigning a named person whose job was to answer dumb questions for 30 days, and saying out loud that asking was the job.

Junior engineers suffered most and complained least, which is a bad combination. We only found out through exit interviews and one very direct skip-level.

A year in, we're still remote. It costs more attention than the office did, spent in different places.
