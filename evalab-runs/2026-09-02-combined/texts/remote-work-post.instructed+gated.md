# What a year of remote work actually taught us

We went fully remote in March last year, and most of what we predicted turned out wrong.

The thing we worried about most — losing the informal knowledge transfer that happens when a junior engineer overhears a senior debug something — was real, but it took about four months to show up. By July our onboarding time for new hires had gone from two weeks to five. Nobody noticed until we looked at the numbers. We fixed it by making people work through problems in a shared channel instead of DMs, which felt performative for about a month and then stopped feeling that way.

Meetings went the other direction. We cut recurring meetings from 14 a week to 4 and nothing broke. Two of the four we kept are now 15 minutes.

Time zones were harder than we admitted going in. Our team spans UTC-8 to UTC+2, and the overlap is four hours. That is enough for a standup and one working session. It is not enough for the kind of long unstructured design argument that used to happen at a whiteboard. We started writing those arguments down as documents with a comment thread and a deadline. The documents are slower and better.

The honest summary: remote work made us worse at the things that depend on ambient presence and better at the things that depend on writing clearly. If your company already wrote things down, the transition costs you little. If it ran on hallway conversations, you are rebuilding a communication culture from scratch, and that takes longer than a quarter.
