**What a year of remote work actually taught us**

We closed the office in March and told everyone it was temporary. It wasn't.

Meetings got worse before they got better. Our first instinct was to move every standup, retro, and hallway conversation into Zoom, which meant people spent six hours a day watching each other talk. In month four we cut the standing meeting count in half. Nobody missed them. The ones we kept got an agenda in the invite or got cancelled.

Writing became the real job. Decisions that used to happen at someone's desk now had to survive a paragraph in a doc. That slowed us down maybe fifteen percent and improved the decisions a lot, because a bad argument reads badly.

Onboarding broke. We hired eleven people remotely and four of them struggled for months — not because they were weak, but because nobody was sitting next to them to answer the dumb questions. Every new hire now gets a named person whose actual job, for six weeks, is answering dumb questions.

Some people were just miserable. Not everyone has a spare room. Two engineers moved into a coworking space we now pay for, and they're better off.

The thing we got most wrong was assuming remote was one policy. It's a dozen small ones: how you hire, how you write, how you decide, when you're allowed to be offline. We had to rebuild each of them separately, and we did it in the wrong order.

We're not going back to the office. We're also not going to pretend the year was free.
