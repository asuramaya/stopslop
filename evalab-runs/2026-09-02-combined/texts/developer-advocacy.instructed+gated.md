Why we're hiring for developer relations

We shipped an SDK in March and watched the support queue tell us what the docs didn't. Roughly a third of the tickets were people who had installed correctly and then guessed wrong about what to do next, because the quickstart ended before the part they actually needed. Our engineers answered those tickets. They answered them well, and slowly, between other work.

That's the gap we're funding. Developer relations here means someone whose full-time job is the space between the API existing and someone using it successfully: rewriting the getting-started path, sitting in the Discord, building the sample apps we keep promising, and carrying what they hear back into the roadmap instead of into a slide. The role is one person, not a team, and it starts in October. We're deliberately not framing it as marketing — it reports into engineering, and the first six months have no talk quota.

The measure is whether time-to-first-successful-call drops from the current median of 47 minutes, and whether the support queue stops absorbing questions that a better tutorial would have answered. We'll look at those two numbers in April. If neither moves, we'll have learned something about our own assumptions rather than about the hire.

There's a version of this job that goes badly, where the person becomes a conference travel budget with a Twitter account and no influence on the product. We've seen it. The protection is commit access to the docs repo and a standing slot in sprint planning.

Our product is an interface other engineers have to learn. Someone should own how that learning goes. Right now nobody does, and the cost shows up in the queue every week.
