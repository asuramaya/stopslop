Engineering Culture

Your first pull request lands in week one. Someone on your team pairs with you to get the local build running, then hands you a small bug in the billing service. You fix it. It goes out Thursday.

We keep teams at five or six engineers with one product manager. The team owns its services in production, including the pager. When the checkout queue backs up at 2 a.m., the person who wrote the consumer gets the page, and that person gets to rewrite it the next sprint.

We review changes within a day. Reviewers ask questions before they ask for changes. If a review turns into an argument about approach, we stop and write a one-page design note instead of trading comments for a week.

We don't track story points. Planning covers two questions: what we are shipping, and what will break. Estimates live in a text file. Roadmaps change when a customer tells us something we didn't know.

You will read more code than you write. Our monolith is nine years old, Python and Postgres, around 400,000 lines. Parts of it are ugly. We fix what we touch and leave the rest alone until it costs us something.

Engineers pick their own tools and defend them in a design review. Two engineers rewrote our test runner last year because CI took 40 minutes. It takes six now.

We hire people who can explain their reasoning to a skeptic and change their mind when the skeptic turns out to be right. Both halves of that matter to us, and we ask about the second one in the interview.
