What a year of remote work taught us

We went fully remote in March of last year with about forty people and a lot of confidence. Most of that confidence was misplaced.

The thing that broke first was not productivity. Output held steady through the summer. What broke was the informal layer: the corrections that used to happen when someone overheard a bad plan at the next desk. We shipped a payments migration in August that three people had private doubts about, and nobody's doubt reached anybody else's until the rollback.

So we made disagreement a written artifact. Every design doc now has a section where objections go, with names attached, and the doc cannot be approved while an objection sits unanswered. It is slower. It has caught four things this year that would have shipped.

Meetings went the other way. We cut recurring meetings from 31 hours a week across the company to 9, and the deletions cost us almost nothing except the weekly all-hands, which we brought back after two months when people said they had stopped knowing what other teams were doing.

Onboarding is still our unsolved problem. New engineers take roughly nine weeks to their first meaningful change, against five weeks before. Pairing helps some. We have not found the thing that closes the gap.

One year in, the honest summary is that remote work removed a set of accidents we relied on without knowing it, and replacing them takes deliberate structure that costs real time. We would make the same choice again. We would write more of it down at the start.
