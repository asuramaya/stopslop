We started a mentorship program on our team last quarter. Here's what it actually looks like.

Six pairs, matched by what the junior engineer wanted to get better at rather than by who sat near whom. One person wanted to stop being scared of the deploy pipeline. Another wanted to write design docs that got approved without three rounds of comments. We asked for the goal in one sentence, and if someone couldn't write that sentence, we sat with them until they could. That conversation turned out to be half the value of the whole thing, before a single pair had met.

Thirty minutes a week, on the mentee's calendar, mentee-driven agenda. The mentor doesn't prepare, and that's deliberate. The moment a mentor has homework, the program dies within two months. We've watched it happen twice at other companies.

The part I got wrong was thinking senior engineers would resist. They didn't. What they resisted was ambiguity about whether it counted as work, which is a fair thing to resist when your performance review is written by someone who wasn't in the room. So we put it in the promotion rubric under technical leadership and named it in reviews, and the pushback stopped inside a week.

Three months later, four of the six pairs are still meeting. One fizzled because the mentor changed teams. One ended because the mentee got what she came for and said so, which I count as a success.

If you're starting one, keep it small enough that you personally know how every pair is doing. Six is about the limit for one organizer. Past that you need structure, and structure is where these programs go to become a quarterly survey nobody fills out.
