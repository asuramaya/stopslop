Twelve months ago we closed the office. A year of remote work taught us four things, and we paid for three of them.

Standups broke first. Nine people on a video call, each one narrating yesterday to seven others who did not need to hear it. We killed the call in March and moved to written updates. Engineers now read four paragraphs in two minutes instead of sitting through twenty.

Onboarding got worse before we fixed it. Our first two remote hires took eleven weeks to ship anything meaningful, against four weeks for the last office hire. We had never written down how deploys work, how to get database access, who owns the billing service. In an office, a new hire absorbs that by overhearing it. We spent six weeks writing the handbook we should have had in 2021, and the next hire shipped in five weeks.

Timezone spread hurt more than distance. Our Berlin engineer and our Denver engineer share three working hours. For the first four months they traded questions across a full day of latency, and a two-day feature took a week.

We reorganized teams so no team spans more than five hours.

Then we found people working too much, not too little. Three of us tracked commits after 10pm on more than half the days in Q2. Nobody asked for that. When the commute disappears, so does the signal that the day ended. We now block calendars at 6pm by default and one manager checks the after-hours commit graph every month.

We are not going back to the office. We are also not pretending the first six months went well.
