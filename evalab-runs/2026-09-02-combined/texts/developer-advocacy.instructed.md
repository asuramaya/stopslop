We're hiring a developer advocate, and I want to explain why before the job post goes up.

Our API has 4,200 monthly active developers. Support tickets tell us where they get stuck, but tickets are a lagging signal — by the time someone writes in, they've already spent an hour fighting something. Last quarter, 38% of tickets were about the same three things: pagination on the search endpoint, our webhook retry semantics, and the difference between a sandbox key and a production key. None of those are hard problems. They're documentation problems that we've been solving one ticket at a time.

A developer advocate closes that loop earlier. The job is to sit where developers actually are — our Discord, GitHub issues, the Stack Overflow tag nobody on the team monitors — and turn recurring confusion into something that scales: a rewritten guide, a working sample repo, a change request filed against the API itself when the docs can't rescue a bad design.

That last part matters more than the writing. When you spend your week watching people fail at your product, you accumulate a specific kind of knowledge that no roadmap meeting produces. We want that person in design reviews. If the third integration in a row trips over the same required header, the answer isn't a better paragraph in the reference.

I'll be honest about what we're not doing. This isn't a conference-travel role, and we're not measuring it in impressions or follower counts. The metrics I care about are time-to-first-successful-call for new signups (currently a median of two days, which is bad) and the share of tickets that are genuinely novel rather than repeats.
