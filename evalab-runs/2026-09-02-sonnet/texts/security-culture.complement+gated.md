Security isn't the security team's job. It's everyone's.

That sentence sounds obvious until you look at how we actually work. Engineers ship features and treat the security review as a gate to clear, not a step in building the thing. Support reads customer messages every day and might be the first to spot a phishing campaign targeting our users, but nobody told them what to do with that signal. Sales negotiates data-handling terms in contracts without looping in anyone who understands what we can actually promise.

Every one of those people already owns a piece of security. We just haven't said so out loud.

Here's what changes if we take that seriously. Code review includes a security pass by the author, not just a bot check before merge. Anyone who touches customer data understands what happens if it leaks, not just that it shouldn't. When something looks wrong, whether it's a weird login pattern or a suspicious email, the person who noticed it knows exactly who to tell and feels safe telling them, even if they're not sure it matters.

None of this requires everyone to become a security engineer. It requires the security team to stop being the only line of defense and start being the group that makes good judgment easy for everyone else: clear guidelines, fast answers when someone asks "is this okay," and no punishment for flagging something that turns out to be nothing.

If you build, ship, sell, or support what we make, you're already part of this. The question is whether you know it yet.
