Investing in developer relations, for a while, meant sponsoring a conference booth and calling it done. That's not what we're doing.

We're hiring three developer advocates this quarter and giving them a mandate: spend most of their time writing code, not slides. Their first job is to ship sample apps and open-source integrations that solve problems our own users hit last month — the kind of work that used to sit in a backlog because no team owned it.

The push started with a support ticket pattern. Forty-one developers asked the same question about our webhook retry logic in Q2, and the answer lived in a Slack thread, not our docs. A developer relations team fixes that gap by writing the docs, filing the API issue, and telling the platform team which error messages actually confuse people.

We measured what happens when a company skips this. Our churn data shows accounts that never talk to a human before month three cancel at twice the rate of accounts that do. A GitHub issue answered within a day, a Discord question resolved before a Monday deploy — these keep people building instead of switching to a competitor with better docs.

The team reports to product, not marketing. That's the part worth stating plainly: their job is to change what we build, based on what they hear, not to generate leads. Success for them next year looks like a lower number of "how do I" support tickets and a shorter time from signup to first working integration.

We're publishing the team's roadmap on GitHub next week so anyone can see what they're working on and file requests directly.
