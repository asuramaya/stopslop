Meridian Freight moves refrigerated goods for grocery chains across the Midwest, and until last year, shipping a code change meant a Friday-night ritual: three engineers on a call, a rollback script open in a second terminal, and a release that took most of the night. The company deployed its route-planning software about twice a month. Everyone dreaded release day. The team switched to Northstar CD in March, replacing a homegrown Jenkins pipeline that had grown past anyone's ability to explain it. Northstar's pipeline runs the same test suite on every commit, promotes builds through staging automatically, and rolls back a bad deploy in under two minutes instead of the ninety it used to take. Nine months later, Meridian ships to production 40 times a week. Engineers merge a fix for a dispatch bug and watch it reach drivers within the hour, not the next release window. The on-call rotation, once a source of dread, now handles maybe one real incident a month. "We used to batch three weeks of changes into one deploy and pray," said Dana Ruiz, Meridian's head of engineering. "Now a single line of code goes out by itself, and if something breaks, we know within minutes which commit did it." The change also reshaped how the team plans work. Product managers stopped asking engineers to bundle features into quarterly releases, because there is no longer a reason to wait. A feature ships the day it's ready. Meridian's next step is extending the same pipeline to its warehouse-inventory system, the last part of the stack still running on the old manual process.
