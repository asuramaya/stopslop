A year into remote work, most companies default to "it's fine, we're shipping." We measured it instead.

Meetings dropped 40% in the first quarter, then crept back up as teams filled the silence with syncs. The fix was routing decisions to written threads first and calling a meeting only when the thread stalled.

Onboarding took longer, because a new hire used to absorb context by sitting near people and now had to ask for it explicitly, which many didn't do. We paired every new hire with one specific person for the first two weeks instead of a rotating buddy system, so someone owed them daily contact, and time to first shipped code fell from three weeks to eight days.

Timezones split the team into a US block and a Europe block, and neither noticed the other's inbox pile up until a request sat for two days. We moved the two biggest cross-team dependencies to Europe-morning/US-evening overlap hours and left everything else async.

Managers who tracked hours worried about slacking. The opposite happened: people worked past reasonable hours because there was no commute to end the day. We now flag anyone whose after-hours activity trends up for three weeks running, and a manager checks in.

None of this required new software. It required watching what people actually did and changing the default around it.
