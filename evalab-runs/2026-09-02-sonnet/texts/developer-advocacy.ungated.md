Why We're Investing in Developer Relations

Every support ticket we close teaches us something a roadmap meeting never will. That's the real reason we hired our first two developer advocates this quarter: not to run a booth at conferences, but to close the gap between what we build and what people actually need.

For the first three years, engineering talked to customers through a support queue and a handful of sales calls. Both channels filter out the truth. A support ticket tells you something broke. It doesn't tell you the workaround a developer found last month and never reported, or the feature they tried once and quietly abandoned. Developer relations exists to catch that signal before it turns into churn.

The job splits into three parts. First, documentation that developers can act on without opening a ticket — we're rewriting our quickstart from scratch based on session recordings of people getting stuck. Second, a public feedback loop: office hours, a changelog people actually read, and direct lines from advocates to the engineers who own each part of the API. Third, code. Sample apps, SDK patches, and PRs into the open-source projects our users build on top of.

None of this shows up on a revenue chart next quarter. It shows up eighteen months from now, in retention numbers and in fewer support hires. We're betting that developers who trust the team behind a tool stay longer than developers who just tolerate the tool. That bet is why the budget moved this year, and why it's staying.
