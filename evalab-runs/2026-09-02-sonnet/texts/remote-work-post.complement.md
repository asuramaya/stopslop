A year ago we sent everyone home and told ourselves it was temporary. It wasn't, and here's what actually held up.

Meetings got shorter once we stopped scheduling them by default. A standing hour-long sync became a fifteen-minute check-in, and the rest moved to written updates people read on their own time. Engineers reported fewer interruptions and more stretches of focused work.

Hiring opened up. We brought on three people last year who don't live near any of our offices, and one of them now runs our infrastructure team. We wouldn't have found them under the old rule that required relocation.

Onboarding got harder. New hires used to absorb a lot just by sitting near people and overhearing how decisions got made. Without that, we had to write things down: architecture decisions, style conventions, the reasoning behind past tradeoffs. The documentation we built to solve this problem now helps everyone, not just new hires.

Trust needed rebuilding around output rather than hours. A few managers kept checking who was online at 9 a.m. long after it stopped meaning anything, and we had to talk through what we were actually trying to measure.

We lost something too. The hallway conversation that used to surface a bug three people had noticed separately doesn't happen on its own anymore. We're experimenting with short weekly video calls with no agenda, just to recreate a version of that.

None of this was inevitable. Every one of these changes came from someone flagging what wasn't working and the team adjusting, month after month.
