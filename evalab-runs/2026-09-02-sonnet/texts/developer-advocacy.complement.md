Why We're Investing in Developer Relations

We just hired our first three developer advocates, and we want to explain the reasoning instead of just announcing it.

Most of our growth so far has come from word of mouth among engineers who found the product, used it, and told a coworker. That worked when we had a few thousand users. It stops working once the people who'd love the product outnumber the people who happen to stumble into our GitHub repo or our changelog. Developer relations is how we close that gap deliberately rather than hoping it closes itself.

Concretely, that means three things. First, better documentation and example code, written by people who've shipped production systems, not by whoever had a free afternoon. Second, a presence at the conferences and meetups where our users already spend time, so we hear complaints and feature requests in person instead of secondhand. Third, direct feedback loops between the engineers using the product and the engineers building it, so bug reports and API gaps get fixed in weeks, not quarters.

We're not doing this because every company in our space has a DevRel team. We're doing it because our support queue keeps filling with questions our docs should already answer, and because the best product feedback we've gotten all year came from a Discord thread, not a survey.

This team reports directly to engineering leadership, not marketing. That's a structural choice, not a detail: the people writing our docs and talking to users need to be close enough to the codebase to fix what they hear about.
