We don't run postmortems as blameless today, and it costs us. When something breaks, the instinct is to find who touched it last. That instinct gets in the way of the thing we actually need: an accurate account of what happened.

Here's the problem with blame. The moment someone thinks their postmortem write-up might be used to judge them, they start writing defensively. Details get softened. Timelines get vague around the parts that look bad. The person with the most information about the failure has the least incentive to share it in full. We end up with a document that protects a reputation instead of one that explains a system.

A blameless postmortem separates two questions that blame conflates: what happened, and whether someone should be punished for it. We only ask the first one. The engineer who pushed the change, the reviewer who approved it, the on-call who missed the alert — all of them get treated as people who did what made sense given what they knew at the time. If that's true, and it almost always is, then the fix is to change what they knew, or what the system let them do, not to reprimand them for a decision that looked reasonable in context.

This isn't about avoiding accountability. It's about locating it correctly. A person who follows a runbook that turns out to be wrong isn't the problem — the runbook is. Punishing the person leaves the runbook in place for the next engineer to trust.
