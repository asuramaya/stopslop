We're hiring three people this quarter whose job is to talk to developers instead of building features for them. Here's why.

Support tickets tell us what broke. They don't tell us what developers tried before they filed the ticket, what they Googled, what they gave up on, or what they built instead because our docs didn't answer the question. Devrel closes that gap. It sits in Discord threads, reads GitHub issues before they escalate, and writes the tutorial that should have existed six months ago.

We tried to get this from surveys for two years. Response rates stayed under 4%, and the people who answered were already happy enough to bother. The developers who quietly switched to a competitor never filled out a form. A person embedded in the community sees the switch happen and can ask why, in real time, before it becomes a pattern.

There's a compounding effect too. A well-answered Stack Overflow question or a clear conference talk keeps paying off years after the fact, in a way a landing page doesn't. Our own adoption data backs this up: the API's usage curve tracks conference talk views with about a two-month lag, closer than it tracks any of our ad spend.

This isn't a marketing move. It's an admission that we can't infer developer needs from dashboards alone, and that the fastest way to find out what's wrong is to ask the people building on the platform, then act on what they say.

We're posting the three roles this week: developer advocate, technical writer, and community manager.
