We're hiring two developer advocates this quarter, and the reasoning is simple: our support queue is full of questions our documentation should already answer.

Every week, engineers write custom scripts to solve problems our SDK already handles. They post workarounds on forums we don't monitor. They file bugs that turn out to be missing examples. Each of these is a signal we've been ignoring because nobody owned the job of listening for it.

Developer relations is that job. Concretely, it means three things this year.

First, documentation gets an owner outside the engineering team, someone who reads support tickets and rewrites the guide before the same question comes in twice. Second, we start showing up where developers already are: conference talks, open source contributions, honest comparisons of our tool against competitors, including the places we lose. Third, we build a feedback loop that reaches the roadmap. Right now, a developer's frustration has no path to a product manager. That changes.

None of this is charity. Teams that adopt our platform and stay tend to have had one good interaction early, a Slack answer that came fast, a sample repo that matched their stack, a maintainer who fixed their bug in a day. We can't manufacture that at scale by accident. We have to staff for it.

The metric we're watching is time from "I found this tool" to "I shipped something with it." If that number drops, the hires paid for themselves. We'll report on it here in three months.
