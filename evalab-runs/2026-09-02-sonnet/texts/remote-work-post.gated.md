A Year of Remote Work, What We Actually Learned

Last September we sent everyone home and told them to figure it out. A year later, the office lease is gone and so are most of our assumptions about how this team works best.

The biggest surprise was meetings. We cut our weekly meeting load by half in the first month, mostly out of necessity, and productivity went up, not down. A lot of what we called "alignment" was people talking at each other because the conference room was free. Now we default to async updates in Slack and save live calls for decisions that need a room full of voices.

Onboarding got harder before it got fixed. Our first three remote hires spent their first two weeks lost, pinging five different people to piece together how anything worked. We built an onboarding doc, set up a buddy system, and opened a Slack channel for new-hire questions. Things turned around within a quarter.

Trust matters more than hours logged. Nobody clocks in or out anymore. We stopped tracking time and started tracking output. The engineers who used to stay late just to look busy now ship the most, because nobody watches the clock.

Some things don't translate over video. Debugging a production issue together, or the ten-minute hallway conversation that used to settle an argument in one sitting, is harder now. We haven't solved that problem. We might not solve it at all, and we're starting to think that's a cost worth paying for everything else remote work gave us.
