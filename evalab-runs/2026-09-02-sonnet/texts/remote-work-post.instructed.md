A year into remote work, our engineering team stopped scheduling standups for 9am. Half the company works past midnight; the other half logs off by 4. We moved status updates to async written threads and kept meetings for decisions that actually need a room.

Output dropped in the first quarter. People spent hours in video calls that used to happen by walking over to a desk, and nobody had built the habit of writing things down instead. By month four, that changed. Pull request descriptions got longer. Design docs replaced whiteboard sessions. The writing was worse at first, then it wasn't.

The clearest signal came from onboarding. New hires who joined remote ramped up faster than the office cohort from the year before, because every decision they needed was already in a doc, not locked in someone's memory from a hallway conversation. We didn't set out to build documentation discipline. It came from removing the option to skip it.

Trust turned out to matter more than tools. Teams that struggled weren't missing software; managers were still asking for hours-worked updates instead of judging by what shipped. Once we tied performance reviews to output rather than visible activity, the complaints about "who's actually working" stopped.

We kept two things from the office: a weekly all-hands, still live, and a rule that any decision affecting more than one team gets written down within 24 hours. Everything else — the badges-in-by-nine expectation, the assumption that presence equals contribution — we dropped without missing it.
