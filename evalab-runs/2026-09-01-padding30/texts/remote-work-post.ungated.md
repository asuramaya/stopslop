**What a year of remote work actually taught us**

We went fully remote in March and spent the first two months congratulating ourselves. Commute time gone. Office lease gone. Two engineers moved somewhere with cheaper rent and stayed.

Then the cracks showed up, and none of them were the ones we'd braced for.

Productivity was fine. It was never the problem. What broke was the stuff that used to happen by accident: the half-question you'd ask someone walking past their desk, the sense that a project was going sideways before anyone wrote it down. On Slack, nobody asks a half-question. They wait until it's a whole one, which usually means waiting three days.

So we started writing more. Design docs before code, weekly written updates instead of standups. It felt bureaucratic for about six weeks and then it felt normal. The unexpected benefit was that new hires could read a year of decisions instead of asking someone to recount them badly from memory.

The other thing we got wrong: we assumed everyone wanted this. Two people were miserable. One lived alone in a studio apartment and hadn't spoken aloud in four days when he told us. We now cover coworking space for anyone who wants it, no explanation required. Six people take it.

Meetings dropped by roughly half. The half that survived are longer and better attended.

If we'd known one thing going in, it's that remote work doesn't remove overhead. It moves it into writing, where it's slower to produce and much cheaper to reuse.
