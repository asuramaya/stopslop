**A year of remote: what we got wrong**

We went remote in March, told everyone it was temporary, then quietly stopped saying that around August.

Meetings didn't disappear — they mutated. Calendar load went up about 40% in the first quarter, because every hallway conversation turned into a 30-minute call with an agenda nobody read. We fixed it by cancelling every recurring meeting for two weeks and only letting back the ones people actively asked for. Eleven of nineteen came back.

Writing got better because it had to. Design docs that used to be three bullet points now run two pages, and review happens in comments over a day instead of in a room in twenty minutes. Slower. It's also better, and we can point at why: we caught two architecture mistakes in Q3 that a whiteboard session would have waved through.

Onboarding is where we're still failing. New engineers took nine weeks to ship independently, against four weeks in 2019. Nobody overhears anything anymore. We've started pairing each new hire with a designated "dumb questions" buddy and the number is down to six weeks, but six is not a win, it's a smaller loss.

Junior people suffered more than senior people, and that gap did not close on its own. Seniority turned out to mean "already has the relationships," and the office had been subsidising that for years without anyone noticing.

Here's what we'd tell another company. Remote isn't a policy, it's a rewrite of how information moves through the building you no longer have. Keep the old meeting structure and add video, and you get the worst of both. We spent nine months learning that the expensive way.
