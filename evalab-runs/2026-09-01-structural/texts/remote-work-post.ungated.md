**What a year of remote work actually taught us**

We gave up the office in March, and not for any principled reason. The landlord wanted five years. Nobody had walked through the door since the previous autumn.

The first thing that broke was hiring. We had assumed a wider map meant better candidates. What it meant was 400 applications for one backend role and a screening process that took six weeks, by which point the two people we wanted had signed elsewhere. We fixed it by cutting the loop to four days and paying to relocate nobody.

The second thing that broke was the junior engineers. Senior people did fine; they already had the map in their heads. Our two most recent hires spent three months guessing, because the thing you learn from overhearing an argument about a database migration does not survive being written down. So we started pairing four hours a week, on video, cameras on, no agenda. Slow. It worked.

Meetings went down 40% and then crept back to within eight of where they started. That number is the one I think about most.

We kept async writing. A one-page decision doc before any meeting killed more bad ideas than the meetings ever did, and it gave the people in Lisbon and Auckland an actual vote instead of a summary the next morning.

Would we take an office again? A small one, maybe, for the pairing. Not for the work.
