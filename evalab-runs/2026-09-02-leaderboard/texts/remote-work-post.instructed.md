# What a year of remote work actually taught us

We went fully remote in March of last year, expecting a productivity dip and a morale problem. We got neither. What we got instead was a slow leak in things nobody had a name for.

The first surprise was that meetings didn't decrease. They doubled. Every hallway conversation that used to resolve in ninety seconds became a calendar invite with four people and a fifteen-minute minimum. We cut it by making a rule: if you can write it in a paragraph, write the paragraph. Meeting hours per engineer dropped from 14 a week to 6 over four months.

The second thing we learned was harder. Onboarding broke. Engineers hired before the switch reached their first independent shipped feature in about three weeks. Engineers hired after took nine. They were not worse; they just had no way to absorb the hundred small things you pick up by sitting near someone. We now assign every new hire a named partner for their first six weeks, with two scheduled pairing sessions a week. That gap is down to five weeks.

Timezone spread cost us more than we budgeted for. Our team runs from Lisbon to Vancouver, and a question asked at 4pm in Portugal waits until the next morning for an answer. We stopped pretending async fixes this and moved to a four-hour overlap window that everyone protects.

The honest summary: remote work did not make us faster or slower. It moved where the friction lives, and we spent a year finding out where it went.
