We closed the Oakland office in August 2025. Twelve engineers, one designer, a contractor in Lisbon who'd been remote the whole time anyway and kept telling us we were doing it wrong.

She was right about one thing and wrong about another.

Right: nobody reads Slack. We assumed async meant writing more, so we wrote more, and by November the #eng channel was producing something like 400 messages a day that roughly four people scanned. We moved decisions into a plain markdown file in the repo. One file, appended to, reviewed like code. Message volume dropped by half and I stopped finding out about architecture changes three weeks late.

Wrong: she said standups were theater. We killed them in September and our bug backlog grew 60% by January. Turns out the fifteen minutes wasn't for status. It was for the "wait, you're touching that service too?" moment. We brought them back at 25 minutes, twice a week, and the backlog came back down.

The thing nobody warned us about was hiring. Two of our four remote hires in that year quit inside five months. Both said some version of the same thing, that they never figured out who to ask. Our onboarding assumed somebody sitting nearby would notice a new person looking lost. No one's nearby now.

So we assign a named person for the first ninety days. Their actual job, written down, is to answer dumb questions fast.

Would I go back to the office? No. But I'd stop pretending the office wasn't doing work we never had to name.
