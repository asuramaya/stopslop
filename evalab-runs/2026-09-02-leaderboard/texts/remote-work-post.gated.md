What we got wrong about going remote

We went remote in March, told everyone it was temporary, and then quietly stopped saying that in August. Here's what surprised us.

Meetings didn't go away. They multiplied. By month three we were averaging 14 hours a week in calls per engineer, mostly because nobody trusted that a written update would get read. The fix wasn't fewer meetings. It was making the written doc load-bearing: if a decision wasn't in it, the decision hadn't happened. Meeting time fell to six hours.

Onboarding broke first, and loudest. Our last four in-office hires shipped their first PR in about a week. The first four remote hires took 19 days. Nobody was slacking. They just had no way to overhear the answer to a question they didn't yet know how to ask. Every new hire now gets a named person whose entire job, for 30 days, is to be interrupted.

Time zones cost more than we budgeted for. Going from two zones to seven sounded like a bigger hiring pool. In practice it meant a 24-hour round trip on any question that needed a human being awake. We stopped hiring outside a six-hour band.

And the thing we braced for, trust, "are people actually working," never once came up. The output was legible. The managers who worried about it were, it turned out, worried about something else.

We're staying remote. Not because it's obviously better, but because we've now paid for the version that works.
