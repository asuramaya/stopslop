Why we're funding developer relations

Last quarter we read through 340 support tickets from API customers. Roughly two-thirds came from people stuck in their first hour: an auth header in the wrong place, a webhook signature they couldn't verify, a quickstart that assumed a Node version we dropped in March. Almost none of those were product bugs. They were failures of explanation, and our support engineers were absorbing them one ticket at a time.

That's why we're opening three developer relations roles. The job is not conference travel. It's rewriting the quickstart so it runs end to end on a machine with nothing installed, then keeping sample apps current in the four languages our customers actually ship in — Python, TypeScript, Go, and, still, PHP.

The metric we care about is time to first successful API call. Median is 71 minutes today, measured from signup to a 200 response. We want it under 15 by the end of next year. If the team doesn't move that number, we'll have built an expensive marketing function and should say so.

There's a second reason, harder to measure. The people who understand our API best right now don't work here. They're the four or five customers who have written their own client libraries because ours weren't good enough. Someone needs to be in those repos, reading those issues, and carrying what they find back to the engineers who own the endpoints. That's a full-time job, and we've been asking engineers to do it in the margins for two years.
