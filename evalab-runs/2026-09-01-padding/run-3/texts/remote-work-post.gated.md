# One year remote, and the thing we got wrong

We closed the office last March. I braced for a focus problem — people at home, the laundry, the dog. It never showed up. What broke was everything that used to happen in the four feet between two desks.

Nobody warns you that your onboarding is verbal. Ours was. The docs read fine to the eleven people who already knew the answers, and were useless to anyone else. The four engineers we hired in the fall took about nine weeks to ship something real; the last person we onboarded in the office took three. That gap was the whole year in one number. We didn't have a remote problem. We had a we-never-wrote-anything-down problem, and the office had been quietly paying the bill.

So we wrote things down. Every architectural call now lands in a dated doc with a name on it, even the ones we reverse two weeks later. Especially those.

The other change was killing the daily standup. Fifteen minutes times nine people times five days is eleven hours a week of status theater, and it existed to make managers feel oriented. We replaced it with a written thread and one long, messy, camera-on call on Thursdays where people are allowed to be stuck out loud.

Would I go back? No. But I'd stop pretending the office was a neutral baseline we were departing from. It was a crutch, and it was load-bearing, and we didn't find out until we kicked it away.
