## What a year of remote work actually cost us

We sent everyone home in March and called it temporary. Nobody has asked for the office back since. But the year was not free, and I want to be honest about the bill.

Onboarding broke first, which is the part we thought was strongest. Engineers who joined before the move were productive in about three weeks. The four who joined after took closer to seven. None of them were coasting. They just had no way to overhear the answer to a question they didn't yet know how to ask, and our docs, it turns out, were mostly a shared memory of hallway conversations.

Meetings went the other direction. We dropped from eleven recurring meetings a week to four and shipped the same number of releases. I had assumed those meetings were load-bearing. They were mostly ritual.

The thing nobody warned us about was decision latency. In an office, a bad idea dies in ninety seconds because someone winces. On Slack, the same bad idea gets a thread, two thoughtful replies, and three days of half-commitment before anyone says no. We fixed it with a rule: if a thread hits ten messages, someone calls a fifteen-minute video call and writes down what was decided.

What we'd do differently: hire a second person into any new role, so nobody is the only one learning alone. Write the docs before you need them. And stop pretending async is free, because someone always pays for it in latency.
