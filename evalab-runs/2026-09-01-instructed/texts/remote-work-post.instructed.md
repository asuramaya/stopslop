A year of remote work: what we got wrong

We went remote in March, expecting a productivity dip. It never came. What came instead was a slow erosion of everything we hadn't written down.

Our onboarding was an office. New hires learned by overhearing. When that stopped, we watched a senior backend engineer take eleven weeks to ship something a previous hire had shipped in three. Nobody was underperforming. The information simply wasn't anywhere a person could go find it.

So we wrote things down, and then wrote down too much. By August we had a 90-page handbook nobody read. The fix was smaller than the problem: every recurring question got one page, owned by one person, linked from the place where you'd hit the question. Handbook usage went from about 4 percent of the team monthly to roughly 60.

Meetings were the other surprise. We cut the standup and total meeting hours dropped 40 percent, which we counted as a win for about six weeks. Then two teams shipped conflicting migrations in the same sprint. We brought back one 30-minute weekly sync per team, and that was enough.

The thing we still haven't solved is disagreement. In an office, people argue in a hallway and resolve it. On Slack, they write a careful message, get a careful reply, and quietly stop talking. Three of our slowest projects last year stalled on unresolved technical disagreements that nobody escalated because escalating felt aggressive.

Remote didn't make us slower. It made every weak process visible at once, which felt the same for about four months.
