We hired our first developer advocate last week. Here's the case that got it approved.

The trigger was a support ticket. A customer had spent three days building a workaround for a limit we removed in March. We announced that change in a changelog nobody subscribes to. Docs wouldn't have caught it either, because docs answer the questions people already know to ask. This person didn't know there was a question.

Marketing can't reach them. Developers have a filter for marketing and it's a well-earned filter, since most of what gets aimed at them deserves to be ignored. The channel that works is a person who has used the product in anger, saying true things in public, including the unflattering ones. That job has a name, and we'd been asking engineers to do it for free on Fridays.

So we stopped pretending it was a side project. The advocate owns the sample apps, the changelog nobody reads, and a standing hour every week with whoever filed the ugliest issue. They report to engineering, not to marketing. That's deliberate. The first thing they'll do is tell us our onboarding takes 40 minutes and should take five, and that feedback needs somewhere to land besides a campaign calendar.

We're not expecting a lead-gen number out of this. The metric we picked is time-to-first-successful-call, and we'll publish it whether it moves or not.

Ask any developer who's shipped on someone else's platform. They can name the person who helped them. That's the whole argument.
