# A year of remote, minus the victory lap

We went fully remote last March. Not as an experiment — the lease was up and nobody wanted to sign another one. Twelve months in, here's what we'd tell a company about to do the same.

Meetings didn't disappear. They relocated. Calendar time dropped from about eleven hours a week per engineer to nine, which reads like a win until you notice Slack swallowed the difference and then some. The interruptions didn't stop. They just got harder to see.

Onboarding broke first, and it broke badly. New hires used to ship to production in three weeks; last year it took closer to seven. The reason turned out to be dull. Nobody overhears anything anymore. A junior engineer used to learn our deploy process by sitting two desks from someone swearing at it. Now they open a ticket and wait a day. The fix was writing. We started requiring a one-page decision doc for anything touching more than one service — cheap, unglamorous, and the single change that moved the onboarding number back under four weeks.

We also oversold ourselves on timezones. Hiring across nine hours sounded like access to a bigger pool, and for about a quarter it looked that way on paper. In practice our Auckland and Lisbon engineers shared forty minutes a day, and every question between them cost a night. We now hire in two bands instead of five.

Would we take the office back? No. But "remote works" is too blunt to be useful. Remote works if you're willing to replace the things an office was quietly doing for free.
